WEEK 02 · INVESTING FUNDAMENTALS

Inflation and purchasing power.

Why having more money doesn't always mean being richer.

15–20 MIN READ

What is inflation?

Inflation is the general rise in prices over time. When prices go up, each euro buys a little less than it did before.

A few percent a year sounds small. But, just like compound growth in Week 1, inflation compounds. Each year's price rise builds on the last one.

Inflation doesn't take euros out of your wallet. It makes each euro worth less.

A cup of coffee

Imagine your coffee costs €3.00 today, and prices rise by 3% every year.

IN 10 YEARS €4.03

The same coffee costs about a third more.

IN 20 YEARS €5.42

Almost double today's price.

The same happens to your weekly groceries. A €100 basket today would cost about €134 in ten years at 3% inflation. Nothing about the groceries changed, only their price.

Nominal value vs. purchasing power

Nominal value is the number of euros you have. If €10,000 sits in a drawer for ten years, it is still €10,000.

Purchasing power is what that money can actually buy. After ten years of 3% inflation, your €10,000 buys only what about €7,441 buys today. The number stayed the same, but you became poorer.

What matters is not how many euros you have, but what those euros can buy.

This is why investors talk about real returns. If your savings earn 2% while inflation is 3%, your balance grows, but your purchasing power still shrinks.

Why does this matter for investors?

Keeping money safe in cash feels risk-free. But over long periods, inflation quietly reduces what that cash can buy.

When you compare savings accounts or investments, ask whether the return is likely to beat inflation. Only the part above inflation makes you richer.

Three key takeaways

  • Inflation means prices rise, so each euro buys less over time.
  • Nominal value is the euros you have; purchasing power is what they can buy.
  • A savings or investment return only grows your wealth if it beats inflation.

All examples are hypothetical illustrations, not forecasts. They assume a constant annual inflation rate.
