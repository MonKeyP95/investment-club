WEEK 03 · INVESTING FUNDAMENTALS

Understanding investment risk.

Why risk is more than just watching prices go up and down.

15–20 MIN READ

01 · LEARN

What is investment risk?

When you invest, you don't know exactly what will happen. Investment risk is that uncertainty: the chance that things turn out differently from what you hoped.

In practice, risk usually means two things. You might lose money. Or you might fail to reach a goal, such as having enough saved for a home or for retirement by a certain date.

Risk is not just about prices moving. It is about not knowing where you will end up.

Risk vs. volatility

Volatility measures how much an investment's price moves up and down. A savings account barely moves. Shares in companies can rise or fall a lot within a single year.

Volatility is easy to see, so people often treat it as the same thing as risk. But risk is broader. A company can go bankrupt, you may need your money at a bad moment, or you may panic and sell after a fall.

TEMPORARY DECLINE −20%, then recovery

Your €10,000 drops to €8,000, but you hold on and it later climbs back.

PERMANENT LOSS −20%, for good

You sell at €8,000, or the company fails. The money does not come back.

A fall on paper only becomes a permanent loss if you sell at the low point, or if the investment never recovers. That is why the same price drop can be a bump in the road for one investor and a real loss for another.

Even investments that have grown well over the long run have gone through difficult periods, sometimes falling by a third or more before recovering.

02 · UNDERSTAND

A real fall: the 2008 financial crisis

In 2008, a banking crisis spread through stock markets around the world. The S&P 500, an index of the 500 largest US companies, fell about 57% between October 2007 and March 2009.

BOUGHT AT THE 2007 PEAK, SOLD IN 2009 −57%, for good

Of every 100 invested, about 43 remained. Selling made the loss permanent.

BOUGHT AT THE 2007 PEAK, HELD ON Back to even in 2013

Prices passed their 2007 peak in March 2013 and kept growing after that.

Holding on was not easy. It meant watching an investment lose more than half its value, then waiting over five years to get back to where it started. And not every market recovered that quickly; some took much longer.

The drift is set so that a typical outcome grows by 5% a year for both. Any single run can end above or below that. The same simulation number always produces the same result. Excludes fees, taxes and inflation.

What the experiment shows

Try a few simulation numbers. Sometimes the more volatile Investment B ends far ahead. Sometimes it ends behind. Its range of possible outcomes is simply much wider, and its path is much bumpier.

Look at the biggest fall from a peak. A drop of 40% or 50% on the way to a good final result is hard to live through. Many investors sell in the middle of a fall like that, and turn a temporary decline into a permanent loss.

Two investments can promise the same average return and still take you on very different journeys.

Remember that volatility is only one part of risk. This experiment leaves out things like a company failing, fees, or needing your money at the wrong time.

Three key takeaways

  • Higher potential returns generally come with greater uncertainty about the outcome.
  • Volatility and permanent loss are not the same thing. A fall only becomes a loss if you sell low or the investment never recovers.
  • The path an investment takes can matter as much as its final value, because you have to live through every step of it.

04 · THINK

One question to take away.

Imagine your investments fell by 30% over a few months. How do you think you would feel, and what would you do?

The simulation and the €10,000 examples are hypothetical illustrations, not forecasts or investment advice. The 2008 figures refer to the S&P 500 price index and exclude dividends.
